# Decision Point Design

## What Makes a Decision Point Work

A well-designed decision point has all 7 of these properties. If any is missing, fix it before proceeding.

1. **Specificity** — Not "what should the company do?" but "Should Widget Corp acquire SmallCo for $500M by the board meeting on March 15?" The decision names amounts, dates, and specific parameters.

2. **Time pressure** — A real deadline or urgency mechanism that makes delay costly. The pressure must be diegetic (coming from the story itself), not artificial. Board meetings, contract expiry dates, regulatory windows, and public announcements all work. "The CEO needs to decide" does not.

3. **Genuine trade-offs** — Every option has real costs. No option is obviously superior. If you can identify a dominant option — one that beats all others on all dimensions — redesign the option set.

4. **Irreversibility** — The decision cannot easily be undone once made. If the protagonist can simply reverse course next quarter, the decision does not carry sufficient weight for case discussion. Capital allocation, public announcements, partnership agreements, and regulatory filings are all appropriately irreversible.

5. **Authority alignment** — The protagonist actually has the power to make this decision. Do not write a case where the protagonist recommends to a board, then have the teaching question ask what the protagonist should decide. If the protagonist advises, the question is about the quality of the advice. If the protagonist decides, they must have the authority.

6. **Data sufficiency** — There is enough information in the case to analyze the options seriously, but not enough to be certain. Students must use judgment, not just calculation. If the answer can be read directly from an exhibit with no analytical work, add uncertainty.

7. **No dominant option** — Students must debate. No option should win by obvious logic. Test this: assign each option to a different student group and ask them to defend it. If one group cannot make a credible argument, redesign that option.

8. **Singularity** — The case poses ONE decision, not a menu of philosophical questions. Multiple analytical dimensions may inform the decision, but the student must ultimately answer a single question: "What should [Protagonist] DO?" If the case ends with 3+ equally-weighted questions, collapse them into one decision that subsumes the others.

   **Example of what to fix:** Instead of ending with "The Identity Question + The Governance Question + The Leadership Question" (three parallel philosophical prompts), collapse to: "Should Amodei preserve the Long-Term Benefit Trust's override authority in the Series G term sheet — knowing that preserving it constrains competitive response but weakening it makes Anthropic structurally indistinguishable from the company he left?"

   The identity and leadership questions become analytical CONTEXT for the governance DECISION, not parallel questions. One action, multiple analytical lenses.

   **The test:** Can a student answer the case question with a specific, implementable recommendation? If the question is so broad that responses can only be philosophical essays, it lacks singularity. Sharpen until the answer is an action, not an argument.

## Decision Moment Ending Modes

The case ending is a **user-gated decision** made during `:plan` Phase 2, Step 2.2a (Case Architecture Gate). Four modes are available, forming a spectrum from maximum ambiguity to maximum scaffolding.

### Mode A — Pure Interrupted (DEFAULT)

The case ends cold at the decision moment. No options, no paths, no alternatives mentioned anywhere in the case body. The protagonist faces the decision; the reader feels the weight; the narrative stops. Students must formulate the problem AND generate solutions themselves in classroom discussion.

**Pedagogical basis:** Charles Gragg (1940) established that cases "contain no statement of the decision reached" and "generally admit of more than one solution." Naumes & Naumes confirm that alternatives belong in the Instructor's Manual, not the case itself. Students should "develop their own criteria, alternatives, and solutions."

**Best for:** Standard MBA (Level 3-5), strong facilitators, cases where the richest learning comes from students framing the decision space themselves.

**Rules:**
- No enumerated alternatives in the case body
- No "Options Available to [Name]" sections
- No "Option A / Option B / Option C" labels
- No structured fork, list, or menu of choices
- The final section uses a Temporal Freeze (see below) to end the narrative at genuine uncertainty
- All options analysis lives exclusively in the Teaching Note Section 5a

**Example ending:**
> Sunday evening, November 19, 2023, Palo Alto — Sam Altman had less than 12 hours before the OpenAI board's decision would become irreversible. His return to the company he founded, the future of Microsoft's $13 billion investment, and the careers of 770 employees all hung on what he said next. The choice could not wait.

---

### Mode B — Implied Tension

The case ends at the decision moment with one final paragraph that acknowledges paths exist without naming them. This scaffolds students slightly — they know alternatives exist — without constraining their thinking by enumerating specific options.

**Best for:** Level 2-3 cases, mixed-experience classrooms, cases where students might struggle to recognize that a choice exists without a signal.

**Rules:**
- One paragraph (2-4 sentences) acknowledging the existence of alternatives without specifics
- No option names, no labels, no structured format
- No "three options" or specific count of alternatives
- The implied-tension paragraph precedes the Temporal Freeze
- All specific options analysis lives in TN Section 5a

**Example ending:**
> Several possibilities lay ahead, each with its own risks and its own consequences. None offered a clean resolution. The partnerships, the talent, and the billions of dollars at stake would follow whatever path Nadella chose. The choice could not wait.

---

### Mode C — Narrative Deliberation

The protagonist's publicly reported options are woven into the final paragraphs as narrative prose. Options appear as part of the story being told — what the protagonist was documented as considering — not as an author-constructed menu. Students are expected to evaluate these options AND potentially reject ALL of them in favor of alternatives the protagonist had not considered.

**Documentary evidence test (all 3 must be met):**
1. The protagonist was publicly documented as weighing specific paths (Tier 1-2 source required per option)
2. The options emerged naturally from journalistic reporting (not constructed by the case writer)
3. Each option is individually citeable to a named publication and date

If any condition fails for any option, that option may not appear in the case body. Fall back to Mode A or B.

**Best for:** Well-documented events where journalism or public statements reveal the protagonist's actual deliberation process. Events covered extensively by Tier 1-2 media.

**Rules:**
- Options woven into 2-3 final narrative paragraphs as prose
- No "Option A / Option B / Option C" labels
- No structured box, list, or formatted alternatives section
- Every option is attributed: "According to [Source], Nadella weighed..." [SN]
- Each option must cite a specific Tier 1-2 source in the source registry
- The narrative ends with a Temporal Freeze after the deliberation prose
- Students may (and should) generate options beyond those documented in reporting
- All extended options analysis (trade-offs, stakeholder impact) lives in TN Section 5a

**Example ending:**
> According to three people familiar with the discussions, Nadella weighed the situation from multiple angles [S7]. He could accept Altman's reinstatement on the board's terms, preserving the partnership but ceding governance leverage. He could negotiate new conditions — a restructured board, expanded Microsoft oversight — at the risk of alienating the returning CEO. Or he could formalize Nadella's standing offer to bring Altman and his team to Microsoft, securing the talent at the cost of the $13 billion OpenAI investment [S4, S9]. Each path carried consequences that would unfold over months. The choice could not wait.

---

### Mode D — Structured Fork

The current behavior is preserved: an explicit options box with labeled alternatives, risk/upside format, and balanced rhetorical weight. This mode provides maximum scaffolding for readers who benefit from seeing the decision space structured for them.

**Best for:** Mini-Cases (Level 1), executive education workshops with limited pre-reading time, introductory courses where students are new to the case method, or when the user explicitly wants structured alternatives.

**Rules:**
- 3-5 options presented in a structured format with labels
- Each option must be genuinely distinct — not variations on the same path
- Different risk/reward profiles across options
- Different stakeholder winners across options
- Every option must be defensible — a smart, well-informed person could choose any of them
- AP1 (Answer-in-the-Case Bias) and AP10 (Dominant Option Signal) apply — no option may receive favorable framing
- Options also appear in TN Section 5a with full trade-off analysis

**Present options in the case body using this format:**

```
Option 1 — [Name]: [1-sentence description of what this option involves].
The risk: [specific cost or downside].
The upside: [specific benefit].

Option 2 — [Name]: ...
```

Do NOT write:
- "The company could pursue a high-risk strategy." (too vague — which strategy, what risk?)
- "Option A is aggressive, Option B is conservative." (framing preloads the reader's preference)

DO write:
- "Nadella could formally announce Altman's hire at Microsoft at the Monday morning all-hands, immediately securing the talent but risking collapse of the $13 billion OpenAI investment and a public confrontation with Altman's board allies."

Every option must name the specific mechanism (what happens), the specific risk (what could go wrong and how), and the specific upside (what is gained and for whom).

## Mode Recommendations by Case Type and Difficulty

| Case Type | Recommended Default | Available Modes |
|-----------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Decision Case | Mode A | A, B, C, D |
| Critical Incident | Mode A | A, B |
| Mini-Case | Mode D | A, B, C, D |
| Illustrative | N/A (analytical question, no decision fork) | — |
| Descriptive | N/A | — |
| Exploratory | Mode A | A, B |
| Series | Mode A per part | A, B, C |
| Industry Note | N/A | — |

| Difficulty Level | Recommended Mode | Rationale |
|-----------------|------------------|-----------|
| Level 1 (Introductory) | Mode D | Students new to cases benefit from explicit alternatives |
| Level 2 (Lower-Intermediate) | Mode B or C | Students begin constructing their own option set |
| Level 3 (Standard MBA) | Mode A | Students extract, frame, and evaluate options from narrative |
| Level 4-5 (Advanced/Expert) | Mode A | Students must frame the decision space, not just evaluate it |

These are recommendations. The user selects the final mode during the Case Architecture Gate.

## Options Analysis (Teaching Note Only)

In ALL four modes, the Teaching Note Section 5a contains a full options analysis. This section is for instructor preparation — students never see it before discussion.

For the TN options analysis, options must be:
- **Genuinely distinct** — not variations of "yes" and "yes, but slower." Each option must represent a fundamentally different strategic logic.
- **Different risk/reward profiles** — one high-upside/high-downside, one conservative with capped upside, one unconventional with asymmetric exposure.
- **Different stakeholder winners** — each option advantages a different group (shareholders, employees, customers, partners, regulators).
- **Defensible** — a smart, well-informed person could choose any option.

The number of options (3, 4, or 5) is set by the TN Options Count gate during `:plan`.

Properties 1-7 from "What Makes a Decision Point Work" and the full "Information State Discipline" section below govern this TN analysis. AP1 and AP10 apply to TN options — even instructor-facing materials must not signal a preferred answer.

## Anti-Patterns (Decision Points That Don't Work)

Fix these before generating case content. These apply to the decision point design regardless of ending mode.

**Binary choice:** "Should they do X or not?" reframes as a 3+ option set. The third option is usually "do X in a modified form," "do Y instead," or "delay and gather more information." Binary decisions produce binary discussions.

**Obvious winner:** One option has all the upside and the other has all the downside. This is not a decision point — it is a quiz. Make every option defensible.

**Authority mismatch:** The protagonist does not control the actual lever. The board votes. The regulator approves. The partner agrees. If the protagonist cannot unilaterally execute the decision, reframe the decision to match their actual authority (e.g., "What recommendation should the CFO bring to the board?").

**Already decided:** The outcome is publicly known. Use a temporal freeze — position the case at the moment before the decision, not after. The case body must contain only information that was available to the protagonist at that moment.

**Abstract scope:** The decision has no amounts, no dates, and no specific parameters. "How much should they invest?" is not a decision point. "Should the company commit $200M to the new production line before the Q3 board meeting, given the supplier contract expires August 31?" is.

**Hindsight visible:** Readers already know what happened. This is not automatically fatal — many great cases are about well-known events — but you must write the case body as if the outcome is unknown, and the teaching note must explicitly address the hindsight bias trap. Instruct the facilitator to ask "What would you have done with only the information available in April 2008?" not "Was this the right decision?"

## Information State Discipline

At the decision point, specify three categories of information explicitly. This is not optional — without this discipline, the case conflates what the protagonist knew, guessed, and later discovered.

**What the protagonist KNOWS:**
- Public information documented at that exact moment in time
- Internal data the protagonist has access to by role
- Statements from stakeholders who have spoken on record

**What the protagonist DOES NOT KNOW:**
- Future competitor moves
- Market reactions to the decision
- Long-term consequences
- Information held by stakeholders who have not yet disclosed it

**What is UNCERTAIN:**
- Regulatory response (pending review)
- Employee behavior (surveys predict intent, not action)
- Partner reliability (contracts say one thing, execution delivers another)
- Market reception (projections with wide confidence intervals)

Never let post-decision information contaminate the protagonist's knowledge state at the decision moment. If you find yourself writing "the protagonist knew the product would fail" — stop. That is hindsight. At the decision moment, the protagonist assessed risk. Write the assessment, not the outcome.

This Information State Discipline applies to ALL ending modes. In Mode D, it shapes which information the structured options may reference. In Modes A-C, it shapes the temporal freeze scene and the TN options analysis.

## Temporal Freeze

The case narrative stops at the decision moment. The temporal freeze is a styled ending passage that conveys stakes, urgency, and irreversibility without resolving the decision. It is used in Modes A, B, and C. Mode D uses the structured fork instead.

### Temporal Freeze Styles

The freeze style is selected during the Case Architecture Gate (`:plan` Step 2.2a).

**The Clock — Deadline-driven urgency:**
Focus on the ticking deadline. Time is the antagonist.

> [Date, time if relevant], [location] — [Protagonist's name] had [specific time remaining] before [specific consequence of inaction]. [One sentence on the irreversibility of the moment.] The choice could not wait.

Example:
> Sunday evening, November 19, 2023, Palo Alto — Sam Altman had less than 12 hours before the OpenAI board's decision would become public and irreversible. Once the markets opened Monday morning, the partnership, the talent, and the investment would begin moving in a direction that could not be recalled. The choice could not wait.

**The Room — Physical scene, sensory detail:**
Ground the reader in the specific place. The setting is the frame.

> [Physical location with sensory detail] — [Protagonist's name] [physical action or posture]. [One sentence on what objects, people, or documents surround them.] [One sentence on what is at stake.] The choice could not wait.

Example:
> In the seventh-floor conference room at Microsoft's Redmond campus, three phones sat face-down on the table. Each carried a different answer from a different stakeholder. Nadella had read the employee letter twice. Seven hundred and forty-five signatures. The choice could not wait.

**The Weight — Emotional stakes, consequences:**
Lead with what hangs in the balance. The consequences are the frame.

> [One sentence on the specific consequences for specific people.] [One sentence on the irreversibility.] [One sentence on the protagonist's position at the center.] The choice could not wait.

Example:
> The careers of 770 employees, a $13 billion investment, and the future of the most consequential AI partnership in the industry all hung on what Nadella said next. Whatever he chose, there would be no quiet reversal — the board, the press, and the markets would react within hours. The choice could not wait.

**The Question — Open-ended, reader-facing:**
End with a question that transfers the dilemma to the reader. The ambiguity is the frame.

> [One sentence establishing the decision moment.] [One sentence on the constraints.] The question was no longer [lower-stakes framing] but [higher-stakes framing] — and at what cost.

Example:
> Nadella had the talent, the leverage, and the deadline. The question was no longer whether to act, but how far to go — and whether the partnership could survive whatever answer he chose. The choice could not wait.

### Temporal Freeze Rules

1. End with "The choice could not wait." or an equivalent one-sentence urgency close.
2. Do NOT write post-decision content after the freeze: "That evening, Altman announced..." belongs only in the TN epilogue.
3. Do NOT use hindsight: "In retrospect..." is forbidden in the case body.
4. Do NOT use post-decision framing: "Most analysts agree that..." is forbidden.
5. The freeze is styled as a blockquote or `.temporal-freeze` div in the HTML output.
6. The freeze style shapes the emotional register students bring to discussion — choose deliberately.

The teaching note epilogue section reveals what actually happened. The case body does not.

## A/B Case Pair

When the user selects "A + B case pair" during the Case Architecture Gate:

**Case A** uses the selected ending mode (A, B, C, or D) and follows all rules above. Case A is the open dilemma distributed for pre-class reading.

**Case B** is a separate document that:
- Begins immediately after the temporal freeze point (the decision moment)
- Covers: the decision made, implementation, immediate consequences, and longer-term outcomes
- Is written in the same prose register and past tense as Case A
- Is distributed ONLY after Case A discussion is complete
- Has NO decision fork — it is a descriptive epilogue case
- Has its own source citations (may add new sources beyond Case A's registry)
- Has 1-2 exhibits showing outcome data

**Teaching Note** when A/B pair is selected:
- Section 10 (Epilogue) is replaced by "Case B Discussion Guide" — how to facilitate the reveal
- Teaching plan includes Session 1 (Case A discussion, 75-90 min) and Session 2 (Case B reveal + analysis, 45-75 min)
- Board plan expands to 4 panels: FACTS | ANALYSIS | RECOMMENDATIONS | OUTCOMES (Panel 4 filled during Session 2)

**A/B pair generates:** `{slug}-case-a.html`, `{slug}-case-b.html`, `{slug}-teaching-note.html` (covers both).
