# Difficulty Calibration

## The 5-Level Scale

### Level 1 — Introductory

**Target audience:** Undergraduate, early MBA, first business course, non-business executives entering a program

**Characteristics:**
- One clear protagonist with unambiguous authority
- Clear stakes and time pressure. Mode D (Structured Fork) recommended — students new to cases benefit from explicit alternatives.
- Single analytical framework applicable (and named in the discussion questions)
- Limited ambiguity — data points in one direction even if the choice is difficult
- Financial data is simple: revenue, cost, profit — no ratio analysis or modeling required
- Stakeholder conflict is minimal or absent

**Bloom's ceiling:** Apply — students use one framework to analyze a situation

**Discussion length:** 45-60 minutes

**Data complexity:** One or two exhibits, no complex calculations required

---

### Level 2 — Lower-Intermediate

**Target audience:** Core MBA Year 1, advanced undergraduate, early career executive education

**Characteristics:**
- Named protagonist in a recognizable industry role
- Moderately explicit decision — Mode B or C recommended — students begin constructing their own option set
- 2-3 analytical frameworks could apply; students must choose and justify
- Some ambiguity in the data — not all metrics point the same direction
- Basic financial analysis: profit margins, growth rates, simple market sizing
- 2-3 stakeholders with different but not deeply conflicting interests

**Bloom's ceiling:** Analyze — students compare frameworks and trade-offs across options

**Discussion length:** 60-75 minutes

**Data complexity:** 2-4 exhibits, basic ratio analysis (gross margin, CAGR, market share)

---

### Level 3 — Intermediate (Standard MBA)

**Target audience:** Core MBA electives, specialized MBA courses, experienced professional programs

**Characteristics:**
- Named protagonist at a genuine strategic fork with real authority
- Mode A recommended — students extract, frame, and evaluate options from narrative
- Multiple analytical frameworks applicable; students must argue for which is most illuminating
- Moderate data complexity — exhibits require synthesis across financial and qualitative data
- Some stakeholder tension — 3-4 stakeholders with partially conflicting interests
- Some uncertainty in the data — confidence intervals, missing data, contradictory signals

**Bloom's ceiling:** Evaluate — students defend a specific recommendation with evidence

**Discussion length:** 75-90 minutes

**Data complexity:** 3-5 exhibits, cross-functional data integration (financial + operational + market)

---

### Level 4 — Advanced

**Target audience:** Advanced MBA electives, executive education, senior leadership programs

**Characteristics:**
- Protagonist authority is ambiguous or distributed — requires inference about who truly controls the outcome
- Complex stakeholder map — 4+ stakeholders with genuinely incompatible interests
- Conflicting data — two exhibits that suggest different conclusions
- Multiple valid analytical frameworks that produce different recommendations
- Cross-functional integration required — financial, operational, cultural, regulatory dimensions
- Ethical or political complexity present (not just analytical)

**Bloom's ceiling:** Evaluate + Create — students recommend AND design implementation pathways

**Discussion length:** 90-120 minutes

**Data complexity:** 4-8 exhibits, requires synthesis across multiple data types including qualitative evidence

---

### Level 5 — Expert / Executive

**Target audience:** Executive education for C-suite, doctoral seminars, leadership programs for senior practitioners

**Characteristics:**
- High ambiguity — not just about what to do, but about how to frame the problem
- No clearly "right" answer even in hindsight — subject matter experts disagree
- Mode A required — students must frame the decision space, not just evaluate it (students without domain expertise will miss key implications)
- Ethical complexity is present and unresolvable through analysis alone
- Cross-industry, cross-cultural, or cross-jurisdictional dimensions add structural uncertainty
- The protagonist's problem is partly a political or institutional problem, not just a strategic one

**Bloom's ceiling:** Create — students design new frameworks, policies, or governance structures

**Discussion length:** 120+ minutes or multi-session

**Data complexity:** Complex exhibits with methodological uncertainty — footnotes matter, projections carry wide error bands

---

## Calibration Heuristic

Run all 6 questions. The highest-matching level sets your floor. Adjust up if the majority of answers point higher.

**1. How many analytical frameworks does a student need?**
- 1 framework → Level 1-2
- 2 frameworks → Level 2-3
- 3+ frameworks, with conflicting implications → Level 3-5

**2. Is the protagonist's decision authority clear?**
- Yes, explicit → Level 1-3
- Ambiguous or distributed → Level 4-5

**3. How many stakeholders have competing interests?**
- 1-2 stakeholders → Level 1-2
- 3 stakeholders → Level 2-3
- 4+ stakeholders with genuinely incompatible interests → Level 4-5

**4. How many time periods are relevant to understanding the situation?**
- 1 time period → Level 1-2
- 2 time periods → Level 2-3
- 3+ time periods with compounding dynamics → Level 4-5

**5. Does the case have ethical complexity?**
- No ethical dimension → Level 1-3
- Ethical dimension with a defensible resolution → Level 3-4
- Ethical dimension with no clean resolution → Level 4-5

**6. Does a student need domain expertise to evaluate options?**
- No — a general business student can evaluate → Level 1-3
- Yes — domain knowledge changes which option looks viable → Level 4-5

## Bloom's Taxonomy Mapping for Discussion Questions

Assignment questions must progress through Bloom's levels. Span a minimum of 3 levels. Never write all questions at the same level — this compresses the discussion and rewards memorization over analysis.

**Q1 — Remember / Understand:**
"What were the three main factors that led [company] to this situation?"
"Describe the competitive dynamics in this industry as of [year]."
"Who are the key stakeholders, and what does each want?"

**Q2 — Apply:**
"Apply [framework] to this industry. What does it reveal?"
"Using the data in Exhibit 3, calculate [metric]."
"What options does [protagonist] have? Map them onto a risk-reward matrix."

**Q3 — Analyze:**
"Construct at least three strategic paths. Compare trade-offs for each stakeholder group."
"Which data in the exhibits supports [position]? Which contradicts it?"
"What assumptions does your recommended path depend on? Are they reasonable?"

**Q4 — Evaluate:**
"Which option would you recommend and why? What is the strongest counterargument to your recommendation?"
"Given the constraints, which framework is most useful here — and which is least? Defend your answer."
"If you were [protagonist], what would you have done differently in the 6 months before this crisis?"

**Q5 — Create (optional, Level 4-5 cases):**
"Design a governance structure that would have prevented this situation."
"Build an implementation plan for your recommended option that addresses the three most likely failure modes."
"If you were advising the incoming CEO, what are the three decisions they must make in the first 90 days?"

Sequencing rule: Begin with at least one Q1 question to establish shared facts before moving to analysis. Never open with an Evaluate question — students who have not yet agreed on the facts cannot productively debate recommendations.
