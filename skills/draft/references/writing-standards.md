# Writing Standards
# Loaded by: atomic-case-writer during drafting and self-review phases
# Apply these rules to every sentence of the case body. They govern register, tense, objectivity, and structure.

---

## Tense Discipline

Write everything in past tense — even if the case is about events that occurred last month.

- Correct: "The company operated in 43 countries."
- Incorrect: "The company operates in 43 countries."

The reason: a student reading this case in five years must not encounter prose that has aged incorrectly. Past tense anchors the narrative to its historical moment and remains accurate regardless of when the case is read.

**The only exception is direct quotes.** Quotes retain the tense in which they were originally spoken. If Nadella said "OpenAI is the most important partnership in Microsoft's history," the present tense "is" stays inside the quotation marks. It does not migrate into the surrounding narrative.

- Correct: Nadella said: "OpenAI is the most important partnership in Microsoft's history."
- Incorrect: "Nadella believed that OpenAI is the most important partnership."

The moment a tense from a quote bleeds into your narration, you have an error. Fix it by keeping the quote intact and ensuring your framing sentence uses past tense.

## Prose Register

Write at journalism grade, not academic grade. Think WSJ long-form, not Harvard Business Review abstract.

- Third person throughout. Never "we," "our," or "you."
- Active voice strongly preferred. Reserve passive voice for cases where the actor is genuinely unknown or unimportant.
- Short sentences for high-stakes moments. Under 20 words for reversals, confrontations, and decision points.
- Long sentences are permitted for context-building and background sections only — not for the moments that carry emotional or analytical weight.
- No jargon without definition on first use. On first use, write the full term and any abbreviation in parentheses: "the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)." Use the abbreviation thereafter.
- No footnotes in the case body. Source attributions belong in the source registry at the end. Parenthetical citations in the body are also prohibited — integrate attribution naturally into the prose ("According to Bloomberg...") or defer to the registry.

## International Audience Standard

Write for a student in any country who may not share your cultural and regulatory context. Apply this discipline throughout.

- **Regulatory bodies:** Spell out in full on first use, then abbreviate. "The Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)" not "the SEC." Same for FCA, RBI, SEBI, CMA, CFTC, and all others.
- **Legal and procedural terms:** Explain jurisdiction-specific processes. "Chapter 11 bankruptcy, a US legal procedure that allows a company to restructure its debts while remaining operational" — not just "Chapter 11."
- **Local business idioms:** Replace with universal equivalents or define on first use. "A hockey-stick growth curve" may be opaque; "rapid acceleration following a period of flat growth" is not.
- **Currency:** Specify on first reference and at every major data point. "$13 billion (USD)" not "$13 billion." If the case involves non-USD currencies, convert to USD at the relevant exchange rate and note the date: "¥1.4 trillion (approximately $9.5 billion USD at the September 2023 exchange rate)."
- **Cultural references:** Avoid unless the case is specifically about that culture and the reference is central to the analysis. When in doubt, cut it.
- **Time formats:** Use month-year (e.g., "September 2023") rather than formats that vary by locale (9/23 means September in the US and the 9th of an unnamed month elsewhere).

## No Editorializing

The case presents the situation. The instructor facilitates analysis. You must not perform the analysis yourself or signal where it leads.

Never evaluate decisions:
- Incorrect: "This was a brilliant strategic move."
- Incorrect: "The decision proved catastrophic."
- Correct: Report what happened and let the data speak.

Never signal the right answer:
- Incorrect: "The obvious choice was to divest the unit."
- Incorrect: "Any competent manager would have anticipated this outcome."
- Correct: Present the options as the protagonist faced them, with the information available at the time.

Never use loaded adjectives without attribution:
- Incorrect: "The chaotic board meeting revealed deep dysfunction."
- Correct: "The board met three times in 72 hours before issuing a statement."

The difference: the second version gives students evidence. The first gives them your conclusion. Give them evidence.

Never use irony, sarcasm, or narrative foreshadowing. The prose must be neutral in tone. You are not a pundit.

## Exhibit References

Every exhibit must be referenced in the case narrative before it appears. Students should encounter exhibits as natural extensions of the prose, not as isolated attachments.

- Correct: "Microsoft's investment structure is summarized in Exhibit 1."
- Correct: "...resulting in a 2.1% single-day stock gain (see Exhibit 2)."
- Incorrect: An exhibit appears at the end of the case with no mention in the narrative.

**Placement order:**
1. Case body (all narrative sections)
2. Exhibits (in order of first reference)
3. Source registry (last)

Never embed exhibits inside narrative sections. Never place the source registry before the exhibits.

## Length Discipline by Case Type

These are calibration targets, not hard limits. Prioritize achieving the learning objectives over hitting a word count.

| Case Type | Narrative Word Count (excluding exhibits) |
|-----------|------------------------------------------|
| Mini-Case / Caselet | 800–1,500 words |
| Critical Incident | 700–1,200 words |
| Illustrative | 1,500–3,000 words |
| Decision Case | 3,000–5,500 words |
| Descriptive | 5,000–8,000 words |
| Exploratory | 4,000–7,000 words |

If you finish a Decision Case at 2,200 words, you have likely omitted context that students need to analyze the decision. If you reach 6,500 words on a Decision Case, you have likely included material that belongs in the teaching note, not the case.

## Hindsight Contamination Rule

The case narrative must end at the decision point. The protagonist does not know the outcome. Neither does the reader.

Never reveal the outcome of the decision in the main narrative body:
- Incorrect: "The acquisition, which would ultimately destroy $40 billion in shareholder value, was approved unanimously."
- Correct: "The board approved the acquisition unanimously."

Never include post-decision events in the case body. If the case ends in Q3 2022 at the decision point, Q4 2022 events belong exclusively in the teaching note epilogue.

Never write with visible hindsight:
- Incorrect: "This decision would later prove disastrous."
- Incorrect: "In retrospect, the early warning signs were unmistakable."
- Incorrect: "Little did Nadella know that within six months..."

Never signal regret or triumph from a future vantage point:
- Incorrect: "What seemed like a minor pivot at the time turned out to be the company's defining strategic moment."

The test: every sentence in the case body must be writable with only the information available at the case's closing date. If a sentence requires knowledge of what happened after the decision point, it does not belong in the case body. Move it to the teaching note epilogue or delete it.

## Interrupted Narrative Tradition

The case body ends at the decision moment. It does not reveal what the protagonist chose, and — in Modes A through C — it does not present structured alternatives.

**The Gragg Principle (1940):** Charles Gragg established in "Because Wisdom Can't Be Told" that business cases "contain no statement of the decision reached by the businessman" and "generally admit of more than one solution." Wisdom cannot be transmitted directly — it must be developed by each student through grappling with ambiguity. Pre-packaged alternatives short-circuit this process.

**The Naumes & Naumes Standard:** In "The Art and Craft of Case Writing," the authors are explicit: alternatives belong in the Instructor's Manual, not the case itself. The case should prompt students to "develop their own criteria, alternatives, and solutions."

**Why pre-packaged options undermine learning:**

1. **Constrain problem formulation.** Students skip the hardest step — framing what the problem actually is — because the options imply a frame.
2. **Create false exhaustiveness.** Three listed options feel like the complete set. Students stop generating alternatives once they see the menu.
3. **Shift cognition from generation to evaluation.** Choosing from a list is fundamentally different from constructing possibilities. The former is easier and less valuable.
4. **Enable test-taking strategies.** Students can eliminate "obviously wrong" options rather than reason from first principles.
5. **Introduce author bias.** The selection and framing of options unavoidably signals the author's analytical frame, even when all options are balanced.
6. **Undermine "no right answer."** A finite menu implies the answer is one of the listed items. An open dilemma admits unbounded creativity.

**The A/B Case System:** In HBS classroom practice, the A case (the open dilemma) is distributed for pre-class reading. The B case (what actually happened) is distributed only AFTER classroom discussion is complete. Students never see the resolution before committing to their own analysis.

**The 4-Mode Spectrum:** The ending mode is selected during `:plan` Phase 2, Step 2.2a (Case Architecture Gate). See `decision-point-design.md` for full definitions of Modes A through D.

- **Modes A-C** prohibit structured options in the case body. No options box, no "Options Available to [Name]" section, no enumerated alternatives with labels. The case ends with a temporal freeze.
- **Mode D** permits structured alternatives under the existing AP1/AP10 constraints (no dominant option, balanced rhetorical weight). Mode D is appropriate for Mini-Cases, executive education workshops, and introductory courses where scaffolding supports learning.

In all four modes, the Teaching Note Section 5a contains the full options analysis for instructor preparation. Students never see this section.
